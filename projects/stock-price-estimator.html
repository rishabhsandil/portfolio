Stock Price Estimator — can quarterly fundamentals predict the next move?

A research project comparing three classifiers — Random Forest, a Multi-Layer Perceptron, and Gaussian Naive Bayes — on whether a stock would close higher one quarter after its earnings release. Random Forest came out on top with the most stable cross-validated accuracy.

Problem

Can the numbers behind a company predict where the stock goes next?

Most retail traders react to price charts; institutional investors react to earnings. I wanted to see whether a model trained purely on the quarterly fundamentals — revenue, EPS, margins, debt ratios — could call the direction of the stock one quarter later, without seeing any price data at training time.

It's a binary classification framed as: given this quarter's fundamentals, will the stock close higher 90 days out?

Approach

Three classifiers, one fair fight.

The pipeline ingests quarterly filings, normalizes ratios across sectors, and labels each row by the forward 90-day return sign. Three classifiers were trained and evaluated under identical conditions:

Random Forest

scikit-learn RandomForestClassifier. Ensemble of decision trees, robust to outliers, naturally handles non-linear interactions between fundamentals.

Multi-Layer Perceptron

scikit-learn MLPClassifier. A small dense neural net to capture interactions the trees might miss; trained with early stopping and L2 regularization.

Gaussian Naive Bayes

scikit-learn GaussianNB. Baseline with a strong independence assumption — a useful floor to know whether the others learned anything real.

All three were evaluated with stratified k-fold cross-validation on the same train/test splits, so the comparison was apples-to-apples. Hyperparameters were tuned with grid search inside each fold to avoid leaking the test set.

Outcome

Random Forest won — but the gap is the interesting part.

The Random Forest landed the highest cross-validated accuracy, the MLP came in close behind, and Gaussian NB sat noticeably below them — confirming the ensembles were learning real interactions, not just memorizing the marginals.

What I learned

What worked, and what I'd build next.

  • Tree ensembles still punch above their weight on tabular data. The MLP needed careful regularization to match a Random Forest that worked well almost out of the box.
  • Sector-relative ratios matter more than absolute ones. A debt-to-equity number means very different things for a bank versus a SaaS company; normalizing within sector materially helped.
  • The Gaussian-NB baseline was the most useful single line of code. It told me how much of the model's accuracy was structural versus learned interactions.
  • Forward returns are noisy. Even the best model is only modestly above chance — a healthy reminder that no single signal beats the market.
  • Next pass: add macro features (yield curve, sector momentum), try gradient boosting (XGBoost / LightGBM), and switch to a proper walk-forward backtest with transaction costs.
